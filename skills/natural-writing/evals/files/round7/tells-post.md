# The tells are not the words

Everyone who writes about machine prose starts with vocabulary, because vocabulary
is the part you can count. Delve, tapestry, leverage, seamless, testament to. Fine.
Put them in a list, grep the list, feel productive. I did that for two months and
caught almost nothing, because the people I was reading had already learned the
list too.

It is worth noting that the list is also where the false positives live. An
ecologist writing about an ecosystem is writing about an ecosystem, and a
salesperson writing about an ecosystem is doing something else entirely.

## What survives an edit

Structure survives. A writer who has learned not to say delve has usually not
learned to stop building every paragraph the same way: setup, hinge, tidy little
inversion at the end. Not the words. The shape.

Watch for the self-posed question and its instant answer. The result? Devastating.
Watch for the false concession, the one that concedes nothing: while the tooling is
impressive, real challenges remain. Watch for three of everything, always three,
never two and never four, because a trained model has learned that three sounds
finished.

While the detectors have improved, the underlying problem remains.

## The leftovers

The thing that ends an argument is residue. A draft that still opens with
Great question! at the top, or carries citeturn0search0 in the middle of a
sentence, or links a source with utm_source=chatgpt.com hanging off the end of the
URL, was not written by the person whose byline is on it. Those are not style.
They are receipts.

My editor found two of those in a piece last March, in a submission from a writer
with fourteen years of clips. He did not run a detector. He read it.

So has any of this actually got better in a year? Not really. The vocabulary moved.
The shapes did not.
