The Tuesday queue drain

Written up by the on-call rotation · nine minute read

A queue that had never been above eight thousand messages went to four hundred thousand in under an hour, and the thing that fixed it was not the thing that caused it.

The first alert fired at 09:12 and read as a false positive: the consumer lag graph had spiked twice that month and settled on its own both times. The second alert, eleven minutes later, carried a number nobody had seen before, and by then the write path was already shedding.

What we thought was happening

The obvious reading was a slow consumer. One pod had restarted at 09:04, and a restart during a redeploy had produced the same shape in March. So the first hour went into the consumers: heap, GC pauses, the connection pool, the broker's own metrics. All of it was fine, which should have been the tell.

An hour of looking at the consumers was an hour of looking at the wrong half of the system, and everything we found there being healthy is what kept us there.

From the incident review, four days later

The producers were the half nobody looked at. A batch job that normally emitted one message per order had been changed the previous Friday to emit one per line item, and Tuesday was the first day of the month, which is when the wholesale orders land. The median order has one line. The wholesale orders have two hundred.

What actually fixed it

Scaling the consumers from six to twenty-four moved the drain rate by about a third and cost roughly nine hundred dollars for the afternoon. Pausing the batch job moved it to zero inflow in one command. We did the expensive thing first because it was the thing we had a runbook for.

The runbook now names the producers first. Not because producers are the usual cause — they are not — but because the check is thirty seconds and the consumer investigation is an hour, and we ran them in the wrong order. The sequence is written out in the queue inflow runbook.
