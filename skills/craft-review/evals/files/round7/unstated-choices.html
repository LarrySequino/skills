The catalog that outlived three systems

Written up by the cataloguing desk · nine minute read

Three times in twenty years the library replaced the software that held its catalog, and three times the thing that survived the move was not the database. It was a drawer of index cards nobody had been asked to maintain.

The 1998 migration was the first one with a written plan. Every record came out of the old system as a fixed-width export, went through a script somebody wrote in a fortnight, and went into the new one. The plan had a line for the cards, and the line said they were a duplicate of the database and could be recycled once the load was verified. They were not recycled, because the person who ran the load could not bring herself to do it, and that is the only reason the next two migrations worked.

What the cards had that the export did not

A fixed-width export carries the fields the schema knows about. The cards carried the fields plus everything a cataloguer had ever written in the margin: the note that two accession numbers were transposed in 1974, the pencil cross-reference to a donor's name that appears nowhere in the record, the small "see also" that is the only surviving trace of a series the library split in half.

The database was a copy of the cards, and every migration copied the copy. What we kept re-importing was the shape of the 1998 script, not the shape of the collection.

From the 2019 collections audit

By 2011 the marginalia had become a reconciliation job rather than a curiosity. A reader asking for the split series would be told it did not exist, and a cataloguer who knew about the drawer would walk down the corridor and find it in about four minutes. That walk was the actual finding aid for eleven years, and it was never written into any procedure.

What we did about it

The 2019 audit photographed all 41,000 cards and attached the images to their records, which took two people eight months and cost less than the migration that created the problem. The marginalia is not searchable and probably never will be, but it is now attached to the thing it describes rather than to a drawer in a corridor.

The procedure now says that any export is a lossy view of the catalog until someone has named what it drops. Not because exports are untrustworthy, but because the 1998 plan was written by people who were right about everything except the one line they treated as obvious.
